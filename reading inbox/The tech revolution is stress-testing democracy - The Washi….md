This week, Britain installed [its seventh prime minister](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2026/07/20/andy-burnham-uk-prime-minister-labour-starmer/c6f4e880-83f0-11f1-9cec-0fb26676f07e_story.html) in barely a decade. France has [churned through](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2025/09/10/france-protest-block-everything-macron-lecornu/) almost as many in just a few years. Its next president [may well be](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://english.nv.ua/nation/marine-le-pen-leads-2027-french-presidential-race-as-europe-weighs-risks-50625558.html%23goog_rewarded) the once-outcast Marine Le Pen. In Germany, the far-right AfD, tiny just a few years ago, [now polls in first place](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.dw.com/en/far-right-afd-reaffirms-ambition-to-govern-germany/a-77840272). Even East Asian democracies — often viewed as stable, cautious, even boring — have seen governments in [Japan](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.cnn.com/2025/10/02/asia/japan-ruling-party-election-explainer-intl-hnk), [South Korea](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/the-perils-of-south-korean-democracy/) and [Taiwan](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://foreignpolicy.com/2026/04/10/taiwan-china-budget-constitution-crisis/) struggle in the midst of growing public anger. And where populists are in power, the situation doesn’t seem that different. Donald Trump now hovers near the [lowest approval rating](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://news.gallup.com/interactives/507569/presidential-job-approval-center.aspx) of any modern American president at this point in their term.

Can today’s democracies function?

Across the democratic world, many countries tax, borrow and spend exorbitant amounts. Spending now averages [more than 40 percent](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.oecd.org/en/data/indicators/general-government-spending.html "https://www.oecd.org/en/data/indicators/general-government-spending.html") of gross domestic product in countries that are part of the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, and the ratio of central government debt-to-GDP in those countries [has more than doubled](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/GCDODTOTLGDZSOED) over the last 30 years. But people don’t seem to think they have benefited.

Why has this happened? Some of it is economic. Expensive housing, [stagnant incomes](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.wsj.com/opinion/what-happens-when-europeans-find-out-how-poor-they-are-270cff5d) and [widening inequality](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.brookings.edu/articles/rising-inequality-a-major-issue-of-our-time/) have fueled public anger. But economics is only part of the story. America has [enjoyed stronger growth](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/NY.GDP.MKTP.KD.ZG?locations=OE) than most other advanced economies. France under President Emmanuel Macron [has lowered unemployment,](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.economist.com/europe/2026/01/08/energetic-abroad-emmanuel-macron-faces-a-mess-at-home) and it has [attracted more foreign investment projects](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/fr-fr/campaigns/foreign-direct-investment-surveys/documents/ey-france-attractiveness-survey-2026-executive-summary.pdf "https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/fr-fr/campaigns/foreign-direct-investment-surveys/documents/ey-france-attractiveness-survey-2026-executive-summary.pdf") than any country in Europe for seven straight years, according to an Ernst & Young analysis. South Korea is an [economic success story](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2018/march/how-south-korea-economy-develop-quickly). Japan was [experiencing a comeback](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-02-20/has-japan-finally-broken-free-from-its-lost-decades-video "https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-02-20/has-japan-finally-broken-free-from-its-lost-decades-video"). Yet democratic governments almost everywhere [are unpopular](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/09/15/how-people-in-24-countries-feel-about-their-political-parties/ "https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/09/15/how-people-in-24-countries-feel-about-their-political-parties/").

The larger explanation is that we are living through one of history’s great technological revolutions. And these transformations always produce deep anxiety and fear.

The last technological transformation on this scale came between roughly 1880 and 1920. Electricity, the telephone, railroads, the automobile and movies overturned entire industries and ways of life. [Millions left farms](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/decennial/2010/cph-2/cph-2-1.pdf "https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/decennial/2010/cph-2/cph-2-1.pdf") for cities. Global trade disrupted local economies. New fortunes appeared overnight, while old occupations disappeared. In this turmoil, frustration and anger found political pathways. The left turned to communism, the right to fascism. What followed were new mass movements, cultural upheavals and world war.

Our own revolution is digital rather than electrical, beginning with computers, then the internet, then smartphones and social media, and now artificial intelligence. The latter might prove most transformative of all. As DeepMind’s Demis Hassabis [said last week](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/framework-frontier-ai-dawning-new-age-demis-hassabis-cngse?trk=public_post_feed-article-content "https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/framework-frontier-ai-dawning-new-age-demis-hassabis-cngse?trk=public_post_feed-article-content"), “We’ve essentially found a way to make sand think.” (Silicon chips are [made of sand](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.asml.com/en/technology/all-about-microchips/microchip-basics).) The ability to mass produce intelligence — intelligence that gets more powerful every month — is surely the greatest technological triumph in history. But that means it could be the most disruptive.

The great political scientist Samuel Huntington [observed that](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://ia801609.us.archive.org/24/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.117282/2015.117282.Political-Order-In-Changing-Societies_text.pdf "https://ia801609.us.archive.org/24/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.117282/2015.117282.Political-Order-In-Changing-Societies_text.pdf") “the primary problem of politics is the lag in the development of political institutions behind social and economic change.” Large-scale change causes anxiety. And anxious societies ask more of politics. Citizens want leaders to restore order, provide security and help them regain a sense of control over forces that seem increasingly beyond anyone’s grasp.

But while today’s technology dramatically increases expectations, it also makes democratic government less capable by comparison.

We summon a car in three minutes. A package arrives the same day. AI answers complicated questions in seconds, and does more complex work that took people months in mere minutes. Naturally, citizens begin to expect similar efficiency from government. But democracy is not designed for speed. It is designed for legitimacy. It bargains, compromises, consults courts, protects vulnerable groups and balances competing interests. Those safeguards are precisely what distinguish democracy from authoritarianism. Yet in an age shaped by technology’s warp speed, they increasingly look like paralysis.

Social media widens this gap. Every governmental mistake becomes instantly visible. Every disappointment becomes a viral controversy. Every frustrated citizen can mobilize thousands of others before governments have even begun to respond.

This helps explain the strange trajectory of modern populism. Politicians such as Trump, Brazil’s Jair Bolsonaro, Argentina’s Javier Milei and others rose by promising to bulldoze through institutional gridlock. Yet once in office, most have [encountered](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.csis.org/analysis/bolsonaros-first-year-balancing-economy-and-cultural-wars) [the same reality](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.wsj.com/world/americas/mileis-radical-new-order-is-running-into-argentinas-old-problems-9559767a) as the leaders they succeeded. Today’s insurgents quickly become tomorrow’s disappointing incumbents.

People don’t seem to have lost total confidence in democracy. They have not turned their backs on elections, constitutions and independent courts. But they are [deeply unhappy](https://archive.is/o/eJFWw/https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/06/10/americans-are-more-dissatisfied-with-how-their-democracy-is-working-than-people-in-other-high-income-countries/) with democratic performance. Democracies must once again prove that they can build housing and infrastructure, modernize public services, manage technological change, reduce inequality — and do so with urgency and effectiveness.

History offers some grounds for optimism. Free societies often look weakest in the midst of great technological upheaval because they move slowly and argue constantly. Yet over time they have usually adapted better than autocracies precisely because they can learn, correct mistakes and change course without violence or repression.

Democratic leaders should not try to outrace technology; they can’t. But they should prove, through relentless focus and competence, that democracies can deliver. Then people will understand that the friction of accountability and the weight of legitimacy are not bugs in the system. They are the features that keep us free.
